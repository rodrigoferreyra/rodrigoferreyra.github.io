Background

Unaric is a growing suite of Salesforce-native applications built to help businesses get more from their Salesforce investment. Its portfolio spans communications, payments, reporting, digital adoption, asset management, and other business-critical applications, serving customers across industries including healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, and more.

Through acquisitions, Unaric brought together products from multiple established Salesforce ISVs with the end goal ofbuilding a unified application suite. Consequently, as the product portfolio grew, so did its documentation.

The Challenge: Building a common foundation

Documentation lived across different websites and PDFs and had been created and maintained by many people and teams. Writing styles, terminology, article structures, navigation, and visual presentation varied from one product to another. Some documentation was comprehensive, while other areas were incomplete or outdated. Legacy content could remain alongside current information, and similar concepts could be explained differently across products.

This created a broader challenge: Present a coherent documentation experience across a growing portfolio of independent products. Besides, these products would continue undergoing development. In this regard, documentation needed to keep pace with them as they evolved, yet there was no established workflow connecting documentation with product development.

The solution: Making documentation part of development

Unaric chose Archbee as documentation platform, giving each product its own space while providing a common foundation for the documentation ecosystem.

I led the migration and adaptation of roughly a dozen product documentation sets. Existing content was reviewed and reworked it along the way. I removed obsolete material, restructured information, standardized terminology and formatting, rewrote unclear sections, and tested instructions.

To guide those decisions, I created a documentation style guide covering voice and tone, terminology, formatting, docs structure, and information architecture. It became my source of truth for keeping documentation consistent while working across different products and teams.

We also established a workflow that made documentation part of product development. Product teams could flag documentation requirements in Jira, discuss specific needs through Slack, and work with me through writing, SME review, and publication.

Result: Documentation that scales with the company's portfolio

The documentation now feels like Unaric's documentation, rather than a collection of unrelated product manuals.

Around a dozen product documentation sets have been migrated or created, with legacy content removed, information reorganized, and existing material rewritten and consolidated. The resulting documentation is easier to navigate, easier to maintain, and visually consistent across the product portfolio.

Just as importantly, documentation became part of the way Unaric's product teams work. What began as an effort to unify the documentation evolved into an ongoing role supporting product teams as their products continue to grow.

Background

Compliance Scorecard is a SaaS platform that helps managed service providers (MSPs) manage compliance for their clients. Its users can implement policies, conduct assessments, and manage many other compliance tasks.

I started working with Compliance Scorecard in 2023, when the product was already in production but evolving quickly. My job was to build and maintain its customer-facing documentation.

The Challenge: Documenting a moving target

The first challenge was understanding the product itself. Compliance Scorecard has a broad range of compliance workflows, and much of the product knowledge wasn't packaged into documentation I could simply turn into articles. To document it effectively, I had to learn by using the product and working through its workflows myself.

The second challenge was that the product kept changing. Screens were redesigned, navigation shifted, features were renamed, and workflows evolved. Documentation that accurately described the product in a release could become misleading by the next iteration.

There was also a tension between presentation and maintainability. At one point, the documentation used custom Confluence Aura components to create a more visually polished tabbed experience in Confluence. While the presentation looked good, the approach introduced problems for documentation usability and made the content unnecessarily tedious to maintain.

The same problem appeared with product demos. I created a series of videos covering the product's many workflows, but as the UI changed, the videos quickly became obsolete.

The solution: Choosing maintainability over complexity

I built the customer-facing documentation by learning the product hands-on and turning that experience into straightforward, task-oriented content. Rather than treating the documentation as a static deliverable, I continuously updated its structure and content as the product evolved.

I also decided to move away from the custom Aura-tab approach and return to standard Confluence pages. From a user perspective, this simplified the documentation structure and improved searchability; for me, content became easier to work with and update. It also made the documentation easier to migrate in the future, since standard Confluence content is less dependent on custom components.

For product demonstrations, we moved away videos toward interactive demos. I evaluated two options: building a lightweight custom demo site or using Storylane. The team chose Storylane because it would be easier for non-technical users to maintain and contribute to.

Result: Documentation built to evolve

The documentation grew from the product rather than around it. It now provides a straightforward, searchable resource covering the product's major workflows, while remaining significantly easier to update when the UI or functionality changes.

Background

Power My Analytics is a marketing data platform that connects data from advertising, social media, and analytics services to reporting tools and data warehouses. Its connectors are central to the product: they allow customers to bring data from platforms such as Instagram and Facebook into PMA, where it can be consolidated and used for reporting. The more connectors available for customers, the greater the market share for the company.

The Challenge: Making connector development easier to navigate

To sustain the development of new connectors, Power My Analytics grow quickly. However, onboarding contractor developers required putting together a reliable source of truth for development information, which existed across Notion, GitHub, Slack, and product demos.

New developers often did not know where to begin, had to rely on existing team members for numerous explanations: From learning about the product and its connector architecture, to the common development workflow and internal conventions.

The problem was not only that some information was missing. Much of it already existed, but it had been created by developers for developers. As a result, the documentation lacked a consistent style and was not always clear or structured for someone joining the team.

The Solution: Turning scattered knowledge into an onboarding path

I reviewed the existing documentation and inspected the relevant GitHub repositories. I also interviewed many SMEs to understand how connector development worked in practice and identify which information new developers needed first.

The result was an internal knowledge base in Notion, with a dedicated section for the connector development workflow. I organized the material around the broader context developers required, including PMA’s product, development setup, team standards, infrastructure, testing, support processes, and internal tools.

The connector section brought the process together in one place, covering the connector library, connector development and deployment, and connector errors and task monitoring. I also made decisions about which information was important enough to include and how it should be presented so that developers could follow the process without repeatedly asking someone to explain it.

Result: A clearer process for a complex product

The knowledge base gave developers a clearer understanding of the steps involved in connector development. It reduced their reliance on other developers for basic guidance, made onboarding more consistent, and became a reference point when team members needed to explain the process to someone else.

For me, the most valuable part of the project was understanding a complex product and translating the way the organization communicated and worked into documentation that other people could actually use.
